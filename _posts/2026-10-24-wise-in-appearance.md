In the *Phaedrus*, Socrates tells a story about the Egyptian god Thoth,
the inventor of numbers, geometry, astronomy, and letters. Thoth brings
his inventions to King Thamus and saves writing for last, presenting it
as a gift that will make the Egyptians wiser and improve their memories.

Thamus is unmoved. The one who creates a thing, he says, is not the one
who should judge whether it helps or harms. Writing will not strengthen
memory; it will weaken it. People will trust marks on a page instead of
what they carry inside themselves, and they will seem wise without being
wise.

For most of the last 2,400 years this story has been read as a small
joke at Plato's expense. And yet, the cost Thamus named was real. Oral
traditions that could carry an epic in living memory are gone. Few of us
can recite a single poem (or a phone number) from memory. The bill
simply took centuries to come due, and by the time it did, no one
remembered there had been a trade at all.

Neil Postman opened *Technopoly* with this same story and drew from it
the lesson that every technology is both a burden and a blessing. Now
consider *when* the cost arrives, and how little it may resemble what
anyone was arguing about at the time.

If technology is a trade, the two sides of the trade are paid on
different schedules. The benefit arrives at once. It is local, concrete,
and felt in the body: the room is brighter, the pain is gone, the work
is done faster. The cost may arrive later, over years, decades,
sometimes generations. It is diffuse, abstract, and usually paid by
people who were never asked.

This asymmetry is why we keep underpricing what we adopt. No one weighs
a benefit they can feel tonight against a cost their grandchildren will
feel. The ledger is rigged by time.

Often, the cost a society fears when it adopts a technology is not the
cost it ends up paying. The feared cost is a decoy. It is visible, first
order, and easy to argue about, so it absorbs all the attention. The
real cost lives in the second and third order effects, in the slow way a
technology rebuilds the world around itself until the new arrangement
feels like nature.

Edison's practical incandescent bulb arrived in 1879, and its benefits
needed no explaining. You could read at night without squinting into a
flame. Factories could run a second shift. Streets could be lit, and lit
streets felt safe. Within a few decades whole cities glowed.

If anyone worried about a cost, it would have been the obvious one: the
candlemakers, the lamplighters, the whale oil and kerosene trades. Those
people did lose their livelihoods, and the economy absorbed them, as
economies do. That was the decoy.

Now imagine telling someone in 1880 the real terms of the deal. In
exchange for your candles, your great grandchildren will never see the
stars. Not really. They will grow up under a sky the color of weak tea
and learn about the galaxy they live in from photographs.

Our ancestor might have taken the deal anyway. That is not the point.
The point is that the price was never on the tag. The phrase "light
pollution" would have sounded preposterous. For the whole of human
history, people looked up at night and saw where they stood in the
cosmos. Every culture built its myths, its calendars, and its gods out
of that sight. We gave it away without noticing we were giving anything.

The pattern repeats wherever you look. The automobile promised to take
anyone anywhere, and it did. The feared cost was the horse trade and the
livery stable. The real cost was the city: we tore out the streetcars,
ran highways through neighborhoods, paved the squares for parking, and
built suburbs no one could walk anywhere from. A century later Americans
pay a premium for "walkability," a word nobody needed when walking
somewhere was simply what a town was for.

The atom promised power too cheap to meter, and the feared cost was a
meltdown in someone's backyard. The real cost was three generations
raised under the standing possibility of instant annihilation, a dread
so familiar we no longer notice it, and one that still sorts the world's
nations more decisively than wealth or ideology.

The pill promised women control over their own lives, and delivered it.
Its bill is still arriving, in mood, in courtship, in the long slide of
the birth rate, and the heterodox thinkers tallying it are still mostly
treated as cranks.

That last one is what the lag feels like from the inside. The people
naming the cost sound unreasonable, because the benefit is so obvious
and the cost so slow. Thamus sounded unreasonable too.

Which brings us to the technology of our moment. The benefits of AI are
already obvious and already felt. It is transforming knowledge work:
writing, analysis, code, research, the whole white collar economy. In
the near term it will move into robots and physical products, and blue
collar work will follow. Every month it gets cheaper and better.

The cost everyone fears is jobs. Will AI replace the lawyer, the
radiologist, the programmer, the truck driver? It is the question on
every panel and in every op-ed.

This is the candlemaker mistake. Treating job replacement as the great
cost of AI is like assuming the great cost of electricity would be
unemployed chandlers. Displacement is real, and for the people displaced
it will be painful. But it is first order and visible, which makes it
exactly the cost society will argue about, legislate around, and
eventually absorb. Labor markets have been rebuilt before. The cost we
can see is rarely the one that changes us.

The costs that matter will come from the second and third order effects,
and those are hard to see precisely because they are not about AI at
all. They are about the world AI will quietly rebuild around itself. If
the pattern holds, the people who collect the benefits will not be the
ones who discover what they cost.

So what is hiding behind the decoy? Two things.

The first, yet again, is atrophy. Thamus warned that writing would let
memory waste away, and he was right. AI offers the same bargain for
thinking itself. Writing let us outsource remembering. AI lets us
outsource reasoning, composing, judging, and deciding. The early
evidence is not encouraging. In a small 2025 study at the MIT Media Lab,
people who wrote essays with ChatGPT showed the weakest brain
connectivity of any group, and many struggled to quote sentences they
had produced minutes earlier. The researchers called it cognitive debt.
A faculty that is not exercised does not hold steady; it declines. We
may raise a generation that can produce anything and understands little
of what it produced, wise in appearance and not in fact, exactly as
Thamus said.

The second is surveillance. This is less a future risk than a present
one, compounding silently. Digital technology has already given us a
surveillance state. Everything you do online, and much of what you do
offline, is recorded somewhere: where you go, what you buy, who you talk
to, what you read and how long you lingered on it. It can and will be
used against you. Not necessarily in a court of law, but in ways you
will never see: the price you are quoted, the loan you are denied, the
job that never calls back.

What AI changes is not the collecting. It is the reading. For decades
the sheer volume of data was its own protection. No agency could listen
to every call or watch every camera; there were never enough analysts.
AI removes that limit. It can read every message, watch every feed, and
correlate every record, tirelessly and at almost no marginal cost. The
archive becomes an instrument.

Today's surveillance state, as Shoshana Zuboff and others have noted, is
felt mostly as intrusive ads and discriminatory targeting, harms that
stay on the screen.

But as more of the physical world is connected, the instrument does not
only watch. It acts.

Imagine the next domestic conflict, fought in a country where nearly
every circuit is connected. Every watch and ring, every doorbell camera
and car, every thermostat, every controller in every power plant and
water system. Now imagine a faction, foreign or domestic, holding a
system capable of reaching all of it at once.

Picture a summer afternoon in a city where every thermostat is cranked
to maximum heat at the same moment. Homes become ovens. The grid buckles
under the load. People cannot stay inside, so they pour into the
streets, and the streets are watched by cameras that know every face.
Then the tear gas starts to fall.

There are a million permutations of this, enough to fuel dystopian
novelists for years. The point is not any single scenario. It is the
category. The surveillance state of the twentieth century could watch
you. The one we are building can reach into your home and act. That is
the difference between a camera and a hand, and we are installing the
hands ourselves, one smart device at a time, because each one is so
convenient tonight.

So is this a warning or a lament?

Warnings assume foresight can change behavior. Thamus warned the king,
and Egypt wrote anyway. Astronomers complained about streetlights, and
the cities glowed anyway. Critics of the pill have been talking for
decades, and the prescriptions keep coming. Read that way, every essay
like this one is a lament written in advance.

But one thing is different this time, and it is speed. Writing took
centuries to show its cost. Electricity took generations. The pill has
taken most of a lifetime and is still not finished. AI may show its
costs in years. That compression could break the pattern. For the first
time, the people who feel the benefit and the people who pay the bill
may be the same people, able to see both sides of the ledger at once.
Thamus's rule was that the inventor cannot be the judge. Perhaps this
once, the judge will arrive before the inventor has left the room.
